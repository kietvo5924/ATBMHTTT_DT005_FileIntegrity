Abstract 
The essence of cryptography is secure communication in the presence of adversaries. We present an analysis of 
cryptography in terms of a philosophy founded on Dretske’s classical philosophy of information, which naturally is 
built on the work of Shannon and is so amendable to being extended with the notion of adversary. The attempts to 
transform cryptography from an art to a science were based on formalizing the adversary, but these very formal- 
izations fall short in the face of real-world adversaries, as demonstrated by the Snowden leaks. Nevertheless, this 
problem is not unique to cryptography per se , but a problem inherent in the process of abstraction itself, and so we 
sketch a research agenda wherein overcoming the limits of abstraction necessitates the embrace of complexity and 
empirical data, as well as the questioning of our own ontological assumptions. 
Keywords: philosophy; cryptography; secrecy; history 
Introduction 
The internet’s promise of universally connecting the world has re- 
sulted in a seemingly infinite space teeming with new and unknown 
adversaries, and cryptography is one of the few crucial mechanisms 
of defense possible in this new world. Yet, while there has been much 
ado about information theory in philosophy [ 1 ,2 ], there has been lit- 
tle philosophical work on cryptography despite cryptography itself 
being the origin of information theory [ 3 ]. In order to bring cryptog- 
raphy to the attention of philosophy, as well as elucidate the possible 
contributions of philosophy to cryptography, we outline the crucial 
concepts of cryptography from a historical perspective, while in turn 
applying philosophical critique to these concepts. Based on this anal- 
ysis, our contribution is then to sketch out a research agenda for the 
broader field of cybersecurity that benefits from the insights and tools 
given by philosophy. 
For most of human history, issues of security have involved phys- 
ical dangers posed by relatively known groups of adversaries, and 
trust confined to small bands of humans, such as the family and the 
tribe. In the last century, the development of large-scale social insti- 
tutions, such as nation-states, has grounded trust between unknown 
parties in explicit social norms and bureaucratic procedures at the 
cost of surveillance of potential adversaries, including its own popu- 
lation, while its own information organization becomes increasingly 
dependent on secrecy [ 4 ]. Cryptography, the study of techniques for 
secure communication in the presence of adversaries, makes the revo- 
lutionary and ultimately philosophical claim that secrets can be com- 
municated in the digital world via the application of mathematics. It 
is precisely this claim that we will historicize and philosophically in- 
terrogate, as it leads to the possibility of new notions of trust and 
security if adversaries can be bound by the chains of cryptography. 
First, we discuss the intuitive concept of a secret and how it fits 
within the philosophy of information as initially theorized by Dretske 
[ 1 ]. Then we turn to how the problem of the preservation of secrets 
in the presence of adversaries led to Shannon’s first formalization 
of cryptography [ 3 ]. Next, we turn to the historical development of 
cryptography and the debates, which were ultimately philosophical 
in nature, over its increasing abstraction, and inspect the theoretical 
limits of these models. Surprisingly, we then show how an entirely dif- 
ferent type of adversary presented itself: the global mass surveillance 
system of the National Security Agency (NSA) of the United States 
of America as revealed by Edward Snowden, effectively bringing the 
field of cryptography to a crisis point by revealing the practical lim- 
its of these formal models. By returning to our initial philosophical 
analysis in the conclusion, we propose that a paradigm based on com- 
plex networks with real-world data is needed to properly philosoph- 
ically analyse the wider socio-technical system of adversaries and 
secrets. 
In summary, we offer more of a broad-ranging philosophical in- 
quiry into secrecy in the digital era than a rigorous formal theory 
of secrecy. Nonetheless, our goal is to lay the groundwork for a 
more empirical and model-based critique of the limits of existing for- 
malizations. This method of construction via critique should be one

Philosophical preliminaries 
In order to begin any philosophical analysis of cryptography, a pre- 
liminary definition of terms is needed. Although our goal is to pro- 
vide insight into the philosophical nature of the adversary rather 
than create philosophical adversaries, we will nonetheless have to 
make a number of perhaps problematic philosophical assumptions 
at the outset. Each of these assumptions can provoke whole debates 
in philosophical fields from metaphysics to epistemology. On those 
debates, while it would best to be rigorously neutral, we will nonethe- 
less have to sketch a minimal picture in order to get our inquiry off 
the ground. In doing so, we will draw on the classical analysis of 
Fred Dretske in Knowledge and the Flow of Information [ 1 ], while 
reverting to the minimalist metaphysical assumptions and ontologi- 
cal pluralism of Brian Cantwell Smith’s On the Origin of Objects [ 5 ] 
in order to deploy Cantwell Smith’s critique of abstraction to cryp- 
tography. 
To begin with a brief foray into metaphysics, the world can be 
considered a primordial metaphysical flux from which local states of 
affairs arise. These states of affairs may be relatively proximal , and 
so capable of mechanistic causal interaction where they affect each 
other necessarily, or distal , such that these states of affairs cannot 
affect each other [ 5 ]. The world can thus be “cut” at various joints 
when analysed in terms of separate systems, although the boundaries 
are often dynamic over time at best and arbitrary at worst. Following 
a traditional reading of Dretske, information consists of regularities 
in these states of affairs that may be transmitted and so lead to inter- 
action between these systems [ 1 ]. 
Information can be transmitted via various information-carrying 
vehicles, often called messages or signals , between systems, which 
may include agents who have the capability of perceiving informa- 
tion through various sensory and perceptual apparatuses. Via their 
autonomous agency, agents take actions upon the world and possess 
a proximal local state that they can access to determine their actions. 
Agents such as humans can be distinguished from mere information- 
carrying vehicles such as DNA and thermometers by virtue of having 
commitments to their own intentional structure: thoughts, plans, be- 
liefs about the world, a commitment to a world as a world, and the 
ability to change the world via their own agency [ 5 ]. However tenu- 
ous and changing it may be, there is a boundary between themselves 
and the world, and between themselves and other distal agents. 
Agents also may internalize information and so can be thought to 
“posses” or, informally, to “know” the information via incorporat- 
ing information into their local state, including via cognition. Rather 
than be led astray in epistemological debates, it is enough to say that 
agents then have access to information when they can incorporate it 
into their local state. Agents such as humans can communicate infor- 
mation to other agents via encoding information as an information- 
bearing message in an analogue or digital manner [ 6 ]. Agents then 
communicate with each other to share information and coordinate 
behavior. This leads to the phenomenon of distributed cognition, 
where agents coordinate their cognition via the “propagation of rep- 
resentational state across a series of representational media”[ 7 ]. This 
serves as the foundation of information organization , where informa- 
tion is shared between various collective groups of agents, and others 
are excluded. These excluded agents may nonetheless want access to 
this information, and are so thought of as adversaries, a key notion 
to which we will return. 
The end result of these preliminaries is that we have the language 
to discuss the nature of a secret philosophically, while still grounding 
ourselves on the traditional paradigm of Shannon information theory 
in which information is sent as messages through a channel between 
a sender and a recipient, which themselves are agents [ 8 ]. With these 
preliminaries out of the way, let us get to the heart of the philosoph- 
ical matter: What is a secret? Informally, a secret is information not 
known by everyone, but only by a few, who can purposefully exclude 
this information from access by adversaries. In other words, a secret 
is information possessed by some agents, but not all, who try to limit 
the flow of this information. 
One can further define the nature of a secret by two conditions. 
First, a secret is a relationship between one or more agents, called 
secret k eeper s , and the information. Second, a secret is relative to the 
adversaries that desire to possess the secret, where any possible ad- 
versaries are excluded from access to the information by the secret 
keepers. Thus, secrecy is a relationship over information between se- 
cret keepers and adversaries, where secret keepers are defending their 
secrets from various adversaries, and the secret keepers and adver- 
saries should be disjoint sets of agents as long as some particular 
information is to remain a secret. 
Any such information itself is not intrinsically a secret per se ; 
what is a secret to one group may not be a secret to another group. 
For example, a map—even if it has the location of underground 
American nuclear warheads in Europe—is not in and of itself a se- 
cret, but only a secret in relation to a set of secret keepers, such as 
the US intelligence agencies who do not want this map to be more 
widely known to their adversaries like Russia. For a secret to remain 
a secret, it must not be transmitted to just anyone, but only to the 
intended recipient(s) who maintain its secrecy and so join the ranks 
of the secret keepers. However, for the sake of brevity, the concept 
of “secret information relative to a group of secret keepers” will be 
abbreviated simply as “secret.”
This brings the question of the adv er sary to the forefront. The ad- 
v er sary is an agent that is trying to gain access to secret information 
despite not being a secret keeper. When the adversary gains access 
to this information, the secret is revealed and the information is no 
longer a secret. The secret only has to be revealed by the adversary to 
themselves, not the general public, and the very fact that the secret has 
been revealed may be kept secret by the adversary themselves. For se- 
crecy to be true, no adversary can possess the secret. The secret keep- 
ers and the adversary are thus in a state of eternal opposition, where 
the secret keepers try to defend their secrets from the adversary. The 
secret keepers may know the adversary quite well: for example, the 
adversary of the American agency in terms of nuclear weaponry was 
the Russian intelligence agency, and vice v er sa . However, the secret 
keepers may not know the adversary is trying to reveal their secrets; 
the secret keepers may consider them harmless or even an ally. The 
Ethiopians originally assumed the Italians were friendly until a trans- 
lator revealed that the Italians were trying to gain access to Ethiopian 
secrets in order to colonize their country [ 9 ]. The secret keepers may 
not even be aware of the success of their adversary in revealing their 
secrets; the Third Reich did not know the Enigma machines were 
cracked by British intelligence at Bletchley Park [ 10 ]. In the extreme 
case, the secret keepers do not have to know that an adversary exists: 
It is increasingly common that an unknown anonymous group may 
hack into a group of computers and that secrets may be revealed, 
but to whom is a matter of mystery . Concretely , an adversary could 
corrupt a secret keeper’s device to access the message, but they may 
also passively record all information sent across a channel, which is 
usually imperceptible to their targets. For this reason, secrets thus are 
typically protected against unknown adversaries.

The philosophy of cryptography

No secrets are eternal; they hold only for a period of time. So 
no information is a secret in of itself. A secret involves a relative re- 
lationship between information and secret keepers over a period of 
time relative to the adversaries and the wider world, such that no 
secret can be absolute. Any secret information in a message must be 
actively kept secret for the relationship of secrecy to continue; the 
behavior and information organization of the secret keepers is struc- 
tured to protect their secret. For example, take the paradigmatic cat- 
egory of classified information, such as US State Department cables. 
Once they were released into the world by Wikileaks, anyone could 
access them and read the information: the cables were no longer de 
facto secrets even if the cables were secrets de jure . These cables were 
only a secret until they were leaked. Revealing a secret does not mean 
that everyone knows the information (as in a leak), but merely that 
the information is known outside the group of secret keepers. 
Once a secret is revealed, it may be kept as a secret by the ad- 
versary themselves or it may be released to the general public via a 
leak , and so the secret becomes public knowledge. It is useful to dis- 
tinguish public knowledge from common knowledge. Lewis defines 
“common knowledge” as the kind of information in a population of 
agents, wherein everyone in the population has reason to believe that 
a particular state of affairs holds [ 11 ]. What we need is a weaker no- 
tion of public knowledge , which is information that any agent could 
potentially have access to, but does not necessarily know (as is the 
case with common knowledge). A secret is the opposite of public 
knowledge and so “uncommon knowledge.” Interestingly enough, 
one of the ramifications of this common-sense view of secrecy is that 
the opposite of secrecy is publicity , such that what is public cannot 
be secret. 
Heavyweight epistemological terms are bandied about informally 
in cryptography, such that an agent like an adversary “knows the se- 
cret” or secret keepers “believe the secret is secure.” Although the 
tricky epistemological question of belief is part and parcel of secrecy, 
a proper treatment of secrecy would be book-length, and so it should 
suffice to note that these terms are used informally to designate a re- 
lationship of access rather than justification or even belief. For ex- 
ample, secret keepers must believe that the information is secret so 
that they work towards keeping it a secret. Does this mean that they 
believe or even know the secret itself? Take the example of the first 
known use of steganography in which Histiaeus of Miletus tattooed 
a message warning of a Persian attack on his poor slave’s scalp in 
a language the slave likely could not read. Was the slave a secret 
keeper? In more modern times, an entire apparatus of agencies exist 
to keep secrets such as maps of US warheads, but not every member 
knows the information in the map. In our analysis, secret keepers do 
not have to personally know the secret (although they may), and so 
secret keeping typically includes a large number of agents who do 
not know the secret but nonetheless work to preserve knowledge of 
the secret from adversaries. Furthermore, secret keepers themselves 
may have access to the information that comprises the secret but may 
not believe in the truth of the secret, such as intelligence of uncer- 
tain or unreliable origin, and such information is often kept secret 
just in case it is true. However, secrecy is not a relationship of mere 
belief, for if a secret is revealed by an adversary, it is no longer se- 
cret even if the secret keepers believe the relationship of secrecy still 
holds. 
While working cryptographers may consider this all an unneces- 
sary metaphysical and ontological excursion, the point of this sec- 
tion is to familiarize philosophers with cryptography by showing 
how cryptographic concepts, once grafted on top of relatively well- 
known philosophical theories of information, can remain neutral to 
questions of epistemology by thinking in terms of access to informa- 
tion rather than belief or knowledge [ 1 ]. In essence, secrecy is rela- 
tive to the adversary, as the adversary is excluded from access to the 
information by the secret keepers. The adversary is trying to reveal 
the secret and, in the extreme case, even make the secret public via a 
leak. The information organization of secret keepers expands beyond 
those with personal knowledge of the secret, to a set of techniques 
and behaviors to defend the secret against possibly even unknown 
adversaries. The philosophy of cryptography can then be thought of 
as a progression of conceptual models of the adversary throughout 
history. It is to this question that we turn next. 
Foundations of modern cryptography 
Cryptography is the study of how to communicate secrets without re- 
vealing these secrets to an adversary. The origin of the term cryptog- 
raphy comes from the Greek kryptos , meaning “hidden,” and graph , 
which means “writing,” so that cryptography is literally the “art of 
secret writing.” So far, we have not discussed the precise techniques 
used by secret keepers to hide secrets from adversaries, and before the 
advent of modern cryptography these cryptographic techniques were 
varied and not subsumed theoretically in a single paradigm [ 10 ]. The 
crucial breakthrough was the creation of the first formal model of 
the adversary, where its powers in leaking secret information could 
be mathematically analyzed. In a move that has confused generations 
of philosophers, this required stripping away questions of semantics 
from information, and analyzing information purely in terms of its 
encoding as done by Shannon [ 8 ]. The motivation for this theoreti- 
cal revolution that led to information theory was Shannon’s crypto- 
graphic work, which was classified at the time [ 3 ]. 
Shannon’s formalization of cryptography 
Modern cryptography can be dated to the publication of “Commu- 
nication Theory of Secrecy Systems” in 1949 in which the terms of 
cryptography were for the first time given a general mathematical 
treatment, with proofs provided for security in terms of probability 
theory [ 12 ]. As Shannon outlines in his “A Mathematical Theory of 
Communication” [ 8 ], the creation of modern cryptography appears 
to be a predictable result of prior published work in defining the 
communication of information in general over a channel between 
a sender and a receiver. The only addition to classical Shannon 
information theory is that some transformation should be applied—
namely encryption—that makes the message unreadable to the 
adversary by transformation into a ciphertext , while the intended 
recipient can equally apply another transformation—decryption—to 
reveal the message. Given that Shannon’s model of information 
is also deployed philosophically by Dretske [ 1 ], the philosophical 
preliminaries in Section 2 neatly map to a conceptual analysis of 
modern cryptography. 
There is a small wrinkle in traditional philosophical accounts of 
information and cryptography; rather than building cryptography 
in terms of information theory, it should be noted that Shannon 
worked in Bell Labs on classified research for the USA involv- 
ing cryptography during the Second World War, and his original 
publication on the formal foundations of cryptography, entitled A 
Mathematical Theory of Cryptography was actually written years 
before his more lauded publication on information theory called A 
Mathematical Theory of Communication [ 3 ]. Therefore, the fun- 
damentals of Shannon information theory, including such notions 
as entropy, were actually developed for cryptographic systems and 
not for general purpose telephone communication as is commonly 
believed. It has little been explored that cryptography is the central

To rephrase the general problem, we assume there are two 
agents—a sender and a receiver—communicating secret information 
encoded into a number of bits. This message must be given to the 
sender in some language “in the clear” (i.e. cleartext , which can be 
understood), but the message needs to be sent through a cipher to 
become ciphertext (or a “cryptogram”) that an adversary cannot un- 
derstand, even if intercepted. A system of communication between 
secret keepers is considered secure if the adversary cannot reveal the 
secrets. For Shannon, the system should not depend on the meaning—
the semantics—of the information, but only on how it is encoded. The 
process of transforming the encoding of a message into ciphertext is 
encryption and its reverse is decryption , where the method of en- 
cryption and decryption combined can be considered the cryptosys- 
tem . The cipher is assumed to be some mechanical system, such as 
an algorithm implemented on a computer, but—as Shannon’s maxim 
states—it should be assumed that “the enemy knows the system”
[ 12 ]. While the design of the system should be public, there is as- 
sumed to be a part of the system that is a secret from the adversary: 
The key used to encrypt and decrypt, and the security of the entire 
system should be reducible to the secrecy of the key, the ancient prin- 
ciple of Kerckhoffs’ principle [ 14 ]. The adversary is assumed to be 
able to commit to cryptanalysis , which means being able to use tech- 
niques such as statistical analysis to attempt to reveal the secret with- 
out having access to the secret key. Shannon assumes that the sender 
and receiver both have secret keys. 
Shannon’s first controversial, yet revolutionary, move is to get rid 
of any concept of the meaning of the information, as he is assuming 
the information is encrypted in such a way as to hide its meaning 
on the level of the bit-wise encoding, and he assumes if the message 
was decrypted then the secret would be revealed and fully semanti- 
cally transparent. These are plausible assumptions given the nature 
of wartime cryptography, although it is less clear if these assump- 
tions are plausible for a full-fledged philosophical analysis of infor- 
mation [ 2 ,6 ]. Using this framework, Shannon proves two surprising 
results that shape to this day the landscape of modern cryptography. 
First, Shannon defines theoretical secrecy (often called “Shannon se- 
curity” or “informationally theoretically secure”) as: “How immune 
is a system to cryptanalysis when the cryptanalyst has unlimited time 
and manpower available for the analysis of cryptograms?” In other 
words, a system is theoretically secret—perfectly secret—if there is 
a way to encrypt a message so that no amount of computation can 
break it to reveal the secret [ 3 ]. Second, Shannon then proves that in 
order for a system to be perfectly secret, the system must have a key 
of equal length to the message itself (i.e. a “one time pad”), which 
means that “perfect secrecy requires an infinite amount of key if we 
allow messages of unlimited length.” [ 3 ]. To the eternal disappoint- 
ment of any simple-minded approach to encryption, Shannon then 
proves that if this key is reused, the cryptanalyst can in theory break 
the encryption. 
While perfection may be impossible, there is a ray of hope, as 
Shannon proposes a new definition of practical secrecy : The amount 
of “work” that is “characteristic of the system [...] taken over all 
messages and all keys with their appropriate probabilities,” where 
the work can be measured in the amount of computation it takes 
to decrypt an encrypted message [ 12 ]. Thus, the idea is that the ad- 
versary must have a finite amount of time and power to break the 
decryption, and so keys shorter than the message must be possible, 
but the exact cryptosystem to be used was not given and the powers 
of the adversary were not formalized by Shannon. Due to the clas- 
sified nature of Shannon’s work and cryptography in general, work 
in cryptography disappeared in the USA into the National Security 
Agency, formed in 1952 and superseding the peacetime cryptanalysis 
done by Black Chamber and during the Second World War by the 
Signal Intelligence Service. 
To take a step back, what is truly underappreciated by both his- 
torians and philosophers is that Shannon wrote his A Mathematical 
Theory of Cryptography in 1945 before he published his much more 
famous A Mathematical Theory of Communication Systems in 1948. 
In fact, Shannon’s classical information theory is simply his theory of 
the communication of secrets purged of any cryptographic elements 
and is not widely taught or even known outside cryptography. Due 
to his original work being classified during the Second World War, 
Shannon was only able to publish his declassified wartime work as 
the Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems in 1949 after his in- 
formation theory gained popularity. In this manner, the entire concept 
of information is a by-product of cryptography, as is the invention of 
modern computers [ 10 ], a historical fact lost upon philosophers who 
tend to focus on cybernetics and artificial intelligence. As philoso- 
phers like Dretske based their work on Shannon information theory 
[ 1 ], Shannon’s formalization of the power of the adversary in terms 
of perfect and practical secrecy fits neatly on top of our initial philo- 
sophical analysis of information. 
Access control and information organization 
Yet even though cryptography disappeared from the public view 
to become the province of intelligence agencies, governments still 
needed control to avoid revealing secrets. The task of keeping se- 
crets between secret keepers can be thought of as access control , 
where there is information and a policy that determines which 
agents may have access to the secrets. While the secrecy of partic- 
ular information-bearing messages may require cryptography, access 
control is the foundation of the information organization of secrets 
in general. For example, there have been attempts to use one-time 
pads to create perfectly secure secret-sharing systems, and for a long- 
period of time Soviet communications required the use of one-time 
pads. Yet, this kind of cryptosystem under Shannon’s paradigm had 
a fatal flaw: each message required a new key. This means that the 
Soviet encryption system essentially had to centralize the production 
of key material in Moscow so that all secret communications went 
through a single point in Moscow, even for covert communications 
between two agents inside the USA. The sheer hassle of the amount of 
key material being produced led to accidental key reusage, which in 
turn enabled American intelligence to successfully decrypt messages 
[ 15 ]. It is not just the techniques of cryptography but the social sys- 
tem of the secret keepers that is required to keep a secret. Adversaries 
are not limited to technical means, they may actively be attempting 
to reveal secrets of their targets by intercepting the message, but also 
monitoring or interfering with—or even subverting—the behavior of 
secret keepers and their information organization. 
The origins of access control systems typically come from the 
military, where secrets must be strictly restricted to secret keep- 
ers, where there is a hierarchical degree of “levels” of classification 
ranging in the US government from “top secret,” merely “confiden- 
tial,” to completely “unclassified”—although additional qualifica- 
tions such as “NOFORN”(no foreign national) complicate the seem- 
ingly simple hierarchy into a more “lattice”-like structure [ 16 ]. These 
policies originated as human-centric levels of access control. How- 
ever, the spread of digital computing sparked concerns that foreign 
governments and other adversaries would attack government com- 
puter systems and networks to gain access to secrets. It thus became

Access control systems with a security model were first formal- 
ized in the Bell–Padula model, which effectively aimed to keep secrets 
between groups of secret-keepers arranged in a strict hierarchy, and 
where agents on the “top” of hierarchy could transfer information 
between themselves and read information of those lower in the hi- 
erarchy [ 20 ]. Another issue was being certain messages maintained 
their integrity, i.e. that agents at a lower-level could not alter informa- 
tion meant for a higher-level [ 21 ]. The more complex lattice-structure 
used by the US government and inside many organizations, where 
different agents can have different levels of access control, ended up 
being formalized much later [ 16 ]. In contrast to cryptography, these 
access control systems are typically enforced via the computer’s ad- 
ministrator or operating system that creates the various groups and 
user identities. One of their most ambitious research agendas was 
to create, in contrast to Multics, a provably secure operating system 
[ 22 ]. 
Three important security properties are typically desired by 
early access control systems, which were then later taken to be 
requirements for cryptosystems. The first and most important is 
confidentiality to ensure that a secret is only revealed to its intended 
recipient(s), thereby keeping the secret in the circle of secret keep- 
ers. There are two more properties that can be accomplished by 
cryptography that do not on the surface have anything to do with 
confidentiality. The second is integrity , namely that the information 
encoded in a message has not been changed en route over a channel. 
The third property is authentication , such that a recipient can verify 
that it was indeed the sender that sent the message. An additional 
fourth property is often implied by authentication, namely that of 
nonrepudiation , so that a sender cannot deny having sent a message 
to a recipient. These latter two properties of integrity and authentica- 
tion may seem divorced from confidentiality, but in fact are crucially 
important to maintaining secrecy. The property of authentication 
is required to identify secret keepers and so separate them from the 
general population and possible adversaries, and integrity is then 
required to ensure that the secret itself is maintained, the information 
contained in the message is not accidental or intentionally lost. Typi- 
cally it should be noted that in the wider security community, the term 
“availability”—information is available when required—was often 
substituted for authentication, although availability is not a security 
property. 
Beyond Shannon’s analysis of cryptography, what is also not 
properly appreciated is the invention of access control modeling as a 
science of information for the organization of secret keepers, as it was 
mostly hidden from the public and only used by governments as ne- 
cessitated by digital computing. However, early access control mod- 
els assume a centralized information organization with the ability to 
identify users and distribute cryptographic secret keys. When used in 
the real world, as shown by the centralization of one-time pads by 
Russian intelligence in Moscow, which eventually led to its failure 
due to accidental key reuse [ 23 ], these systems quickly became cum- 
bersome and difficult to deploy in a ever-changing dynamic world of 
secrets and adversaries. 
Formalizing the adversary 
Two philosophical questions bedevilled the use of cryptography. The 
first was how cryptography could be used by secret keepers that were 
not part of a centralized organization that could deliver secret keys. 
The second question was how could it be guaranteed that a real- 
world adversary, as opposed to the perfect adversary imagined by 
Shannon, could not break a particular cryptographic encoding of a 
message by secret keepers. 
The revolution of public key cryptography 
Up until 1976, cryptosystems were symmetric key-based systems of 
the type formalized by Shannon, where the sender and receiver used 
a secret key for both decryption and encryption. Yet for messages to 
be encrypted and decrypted by a secret key, both the receiver and the 
sender had to have their secret key somehow securely given to them 
via some secure channel. In other words, it was generally assumed 
that the keys were distributed by a trusted third-party in the larger 
organization of secret keepers. A trusted third-party is an element of 
a communication protocol that is trusted not to reveal any secret. 
In practice, this tended to limit the use of cryptography in practice 
to centralized social organizations such as governments with nec- 
essary infrastructures. This, and the fact that most cryptographers 
were employed by intelligence agencies, meant that the public aca- 
demic field of cryptography barely existed, although the publication 
of Kahn’s The Codebreak er s in 1967 renewed interest in the devel- 
opment of a possible science of secrets [ 10 ]. Academics such as Whit- 
field Diffie and Martin Hellman began to question whether or not the 
usage of cryptography was possible over insecure channels by multi- 
ple users when observed by an adversary without preexisting secret 
keys [ 24 ].1 
The invention of public-key cryptography by Diffie and Hellman 
in their New Directions in Cryptography [ 25 ] solved the problem 
of key distribution amongst an open-ended number of parties over 
insecure channels via the creation of public key cryptography . Their 
central insight was to “split”the key into a public key and private key, 
where two users could then exchange public keys and, via the use of 
their private key, create a new shared secret key to secure the chan- 
nel even with the adversary intercepting the public keys and view- 
ing all ciphertext messages. This would allow any person, even those 
without organizations, to become a secret keeper and use cryptogra- 
phy to protect their secrets from adversaries. The philosophical and 
even political ramifications of the historic shift in the landscape of 
sovereignty caused by this breakthrough have yet to be fully appre- 
ciated [ 4 ]. 
Earlier, Shannon enigmatically ended his original essay by noting 
that “the problem of good cipher design is essentially one of finding 
difficult problems” [ 12 ]. Building on this insight, Diffie and Hellman 
discovered that certain mathematical functions had “trap door” in- 
formation that allowed an agent with this secret information (such 
as a key) to reverse a function. Without such information it was im- 
possible, however, and these “one way” functions could be used to 
build new types of public key cryptosystems, where the private key 
was the “trap door.” As Jacques Stern, the founding figure of French 
cryptography, put it: “from an epistemological perspective, one can 
say that Diffie and Hellman have drawn the most extreme conse- 
quence” from Kerckhoffs’ principle so that the encrypting key can be 
public and only the decrypting key must be secret [ 26 ]. The public

Diffie–Hellman key exchange neatly solved the problem of key 
distribution without a trusted third party, but it failed to actually 
provide algorithms to encrypt and decrypt messages using public 
key cryptography. While Diffie and Hellman did list a number of 
attacks—such as the adversary being able to send arbitrary plain-

sessing the decrypted plaintext for arbitrary ciphertexts (the chosen 
ciphertext attack)—their ground-breaking work also did not provide 
a method for determining whether an encryption algorithm was se-

some problems that seemed to be hard “trapdoor” type problems 
for worst-case scenarios, such as Merkle’s knapsacks [ 27 ], would fail 
against actual adversaries who possessed “average-case” computa-

by adversaries. After all, there is no reason to believe that adversaries 
would limit themselves to known attacks. 
Diffie and Hellman stated that “we stand today on the brink of 
a revolution in cryptography,” and the revolution they began was 
completed by the invention of the Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (R S A) 
cryptosystem that could encrypt, decrypt, and also sign and verify 
digital signatures using the factorization of the product of two large 
prime numbers as the trapdoor function [ 29 ]. Further work by Hell- 
man’s student El-Gamal then extended the work of Diffie–Hellman 
Key Exchange to provide encryption and decryption-based on mod- 
ular exponentiation. Yet, even these cryptosystems effectively were 
not proven to be secure using mathematical proofs. Attempts to con- 
struct a R S A cryptosystem based on standard assumptions, such as 
the provably secure Rabin cryptosystem [ 30 ], discovered that the very 
properties that made it provably secure could be provably broken by 
chosen ciphertext attacks [ 31 ]. These problems could be solved by 
the introduction of randomness into the decryption and encryption 
operations themselves. This would include not just the keys, but also 
randomness appended to the message before encryption [ 32 ]. Yet the 
exact key sizes, padding, and other matters appeared to be more an 
art than a science. 
Furthermore, real deployed cryptosystems needed to have these 
parameters set in order to interoperate. After at first attempting to 
suppress the spread of public-key cryptography, the NSA eventu- 
ally began to work with National Institute of Standards and Tech- 
nology (NIST) to standardize cryptosystems for use outside govern- 
ments. This led to the distinction between cryptographic primitives , 
the components of a cryptosystem whose function is assumed to pro- 
vide a security property, against cryptographic protocols that are 
constructed out of cryptographic primitives to achieve a more com- 
plex function in a secure manner. Examples of cryptographic prim- 
itives include hash functions used to create cryptographic one-way 
“digests” (similar to a unique fingerprint for an encoding of infor- 
mation), ranging from Secure Hashing Algorithm (SHA) to symmet- 
ric encryption algorithms like Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), 
as well as public key systems for encryption, decryption, and digi- 
tal signatures. As cryptography spread, the standardization of these 
primitives was led by nation-state level standards bodies that were 
closely connected to their respective intelligence agencies in terms 
of cryptography, such as NIST (USA), CRYPTREC (Japan), GOST 
(Russia), and ETSI (Europe), with standards from the USA domi- 
nating deployments. In the early days of cryptographic systems, na- 
tion standards were done in an opaque manner, as exemplified by 
the production of Digital Encryption Standard (DES) by IBM with 
NSA approval and Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA), an algorithm 
that was purposefully crippled to prevent confidentiality (which the 
NSA viewed as dangerous at the time to spread to the public, though 
it allowed authentication). The USA moved to running open con- 
tests like NIST, where cryptographers attacked each other’s propos- 
als for primitives. These were produced via cryptographic contests 
ran by NIST that nonetheless left the question open: How do we 
know the power of the adversary, and can we prevent unknown 
attacks? 
Formalizing the adversary via computational 
complexity 
The philosophical answer was to formalize the adversary on a level 
of abstraction so that all possible attacks could be precisely charac- 
terized in terms of the theory of computation, and security could be 
proven against such an abstract adversary. The advent of provable 
security was began by Shafi Goldwasser and Silvio Micali’s precise 
mathematical formalization of the adversary in terms of computa- 
tional complexity theory, which could characterize an adversary with 
a bounded amount of computation as a particular kind of Turing ma- 
chine operating over binary encoded messages [ 33 ]. One way for the 
adversary to break any cryptosystem is simply to enumerate all pos- 
sible keys, which on a classical (super)computer still takes an expo- 
nential amount of time, and so would not terminate in thousands of 
years. Thus, an exponential–time algorithm to break a cryptosystem 
is as good as not having an algorithm for the purposes of practi- 
cal security. In order to formalize this insight, the adversary can be 
given a Turing machine that runs in polynomial time; its running 
time is upper bounded by a polynomial expression in the size of the 
input, where the input may be any observable information given by 
the sending of the encrypted message between the sender and the re- 
ceiver [ 34 ]. The adversary can also be considered to have access to 
uniformly chosen and independent randomness, and so is a prob- 
abilistic polynomial–time Turing machine (PPT). To generalize, for 
each possible input size, the adversary could possess a different al- 
gorithm, so the adversary is a nonuniform PPT. A computationally 
secure cryptosystem requires proving that a cryptosystem could be re- 
duced to the computational intractability of a mathematical problem. 
The proof typically produced bounds on the success of the nonuni- 
form PPT adversary, with a system being secure if the probability of 
success was shown to be negligible within a bound. 
Via this breakthrough, previously informally characterized at- 
tacks could be reformulated mathematically, and so systems could 
be proven to be secure in terms of cryptographic properties like con- 
fidentiality. For example, chosen plaintext attacks could be rephrased 
mathematically via semantic security in terms of the polynomial–time 
(efficient) adversary: “Whatever is efficiently computable about the 
cleartext given the ciphertext, is also efficiently computable without 
the ciphertext” [ 34 ]. While some probabilistic advantage of the ad- 
versary must exist because the plaintext is mixed into the ciphertext 
by the key, it is bounded to be negligible. This notion could be for- 
malized into the formal property of indistinguishability in the face 
of plaintext and ciphertext attacks, with the eventual result that a 
particular cryptosystem could be provably secure against these kinds 
of attacks.

gram for secrecy, becoming as respectable as any other branch 
of mathematics, a feat made possible through replacing paranoid

Cryptographers would use “games” to define reductions to known

Could cryptography transform from a mathematical discipline 
to a scientific method with real-world impact? Provable security 
was theorized to have potentially huge ramifications for the prac-

needed common encoding and padding to be used in practice se- 
curely, so new standards like Public Key Cryptography Standards 
(PKCS) #1 were drafted [ 36 ]. Standards could depend on practice- 
oriented provable security , where the security properties of various 
deployed standards could be studied rigorously and proven [ 37 ]. Yet 
with a few exceptions, such as the Cramer–Schoup cryptosystem 
[ 38 ], most cryptosystems—even R S A—could not be proven secure 
within a standard computational model [ 39 ]. 
The core of the problem was that many real-world primitives, 
such as hash functions, were not clearly definable mathematically 
under the standard model of cryptography. Therefore, there were 
new extensions to provable security that substituted the ideal 
functionality in proofs for the use of a standard primitive in the real 
world. For example, the SHA1 hash function could be modeled as 
a “random oracle model” in order to be used in proofs, where the 
random oracle maps each possible input to a random output whose 
infinite output could then be truncated [ 40 ]. Likewise, proofs could 
be made in the generic group model, where an encoding that was 
efficient (such as the finite fields used by R S A) could be substituted 
with a random encoding [ 41 ]. Trusted third-parties whose behav- 
ior was considered nonadversarial could also be invoked for the 
need in proofs, such as the setup of a common reference string in 
zero-knowledge proofs [ 42 ]. 
Progress in formalization led to a veritable blossoming of a thou- 
sand proofs, with proofs for useful new constructions and proofs 
of existing protocols becoming the bread and butter of academic 
cryptography. Previously unsolved problems could be solved, with 
the provably secure optimal padding for R S A being put forward for 
standardization [ 32 ]. Going even further, combinations of protocols 
could even be proven secure using proof techniques such as universal 
composabilty, where one could define the functionality in an “ideal 
world” and prove that a “real world” instantiation fulfilled certain 
security and privacy properties [ 43 ]. Work on indistinguishability ob- 
fuscation held the promise of being able to construct all the diverse 
branches of cryptography with the addition of “one-way” functions 
[ 44 ]. Large-scale work began systematizing a formal treatment of 
cryptography based in complexity theory [ 45 ]. For readers wishing to 
learn cryptography, textbooks on cryptography based on precise def- 
initions with proofs are available [ 46 ]. The hope was that the design 
of cryptographic protocols could, by building on formally provable 
security, transform cryptography from an informal art to a branch of 
mathematics on par with any other [ 37 ]. 
Formalizing the adversary via logic 
The nascent field of security engineering formed to analyze not just 
cryptography, but cryptography as part of the construction of larger 
systems of information organization. The revolution of public key 
cryptography allowed new decentralized models of access control, 
which became exceptionally important due to the increased adoption 
of the internet. Unlike previous access-control systems such as the 
Biba model, which assumed a finite number of secret keepers [ 21 ], 
the internet is an open world containing a vast number of different 
kinds of adversaries and secret keepers. In this new environment, a 
system needs to distinguish secret keepers from possible adversaries. 
This insight gave rise to the field of authentication protocols , where 
an agent could prove using cryptography that they are a legitimate 
secret keeper to a larger information organization. 
The first use-cases were authentication between agents, each with 
a symmetric key on a trust server, in the face of communicating in the 
presence of an adversary attempting breaches of integrity and confi- 
dentiality, as well as “replay”attacks, where messages were held back 
or forwarded by the adversary. A host of strangely named home- 
brewed protocols, such as the Wide-Mouthed Frog and Yahalom 
protocols, were shown to be insecure upon closer inspection [ 47 ]. 
This led to the more rigorous Needham–Schroeder protocol for key 
distribution [ 47 ], which in turn became the basis for Kerberos, the 
first widely used authentication system on the internet [ 48 ]. Still, it 
was not clear if even the Needham–Schroeder protocol was secure. 
A number of attacks led to features being added in an ad hoc man- 
ner to the protocol, like “timestamps” to prevent replay attacks [ 49 ]. 
To address these issues, a revolution in formalization took place in 
the study of the security of these authentication protocols, strangely 
in parallel to the formalization of cryptography as it was led by a 
separate research community [ 50 ]. As opposed to the formalization 
of cryptography, where “cryptographic operations are seen as func- 
tions on strings of bits” and their security properties are “defined in 
terms of the probability and computational complexity of successful 
attacks,” in the logic-based paradigm used by access control systems, 
cryptographic operations and their security properties “are seen as 
functions on a space of symbolic (formal) expressions” [ 50 ]. 
The general approach was that the communication of secrets 
could be studied in terms of the beliefs about the messages by both 
secret keepers and adversaries. Prior to the invention of cryptogra- 
phy, this was formalized in terms of epistemic logic, whose roots 
were in philosophy [ 51 ]. In a logic-based analysis, agents are ab- 
stract machines that can send and receive messages. In contrast to 
the investigation of the correctness of cryptosystems, cryptographic 
operations are assumed to be perfectly correct. However, the adver- 
sary is characterized in a completely different manner: Rather than 
a polynomial–time Turing machine, under the Dolev–Yao symbolic 
model the adversary is extremely powerful and “carries the message”
over a hostile network, where the adversary can intercept, alter, and 
generate any message [ 52 ]. 
The first influential logic for authentication protocols, Burrows–
Abadi–Needham (BAN) logic, applied an epistemic logic-based 
approach to existing authentication protocols, showing that the 
Needham–Schroeder was “secure” [ 53 ]. However, BAN logic lacked 
a standard model-theoretic semantics of possible universes, and pro- 
tocols proven secure by BAN logic in theory were found to be 
actually insecure in practice due to the level of abstraction and limits 
in the logic itself [ 54 ]. In the wake of these deep-seated problems of 
BAN logic, researchers created a panoply of new logics: some with 
soundness properties [ 55 ], others based on the pi-calculus [ 56 ], still 
others on strand spaces [ 57 ], and well-defined multiagent epistemic 
logics [ 58 ]. The eventual victory of finding a suitable and more per- 
fect logic appeared to be assured, and the unification of logic-based

One advantage of being able to capture the philosophical notion 
of secrecy in standard logics is that protocols could then in theory be 
amendable to automated theorem-proving, where a machine rather 
than a human cryptographer generates the proofs using an automatic 
model-checker [ 60 ]. The core idea was that a run of the protocol, 
and all attacks by an adversary against it, could be conceptualized as 
moves in a space of all possible states of the protocol. A human cryp- 
tographer, with finite attention, was only likely to explore a relatively 
small subset of the space and thus only able to notice a few possi- 
ble attacks. Alternatively, a machine could explore much more of the 
state space automatically—if not the entire state space—and find oth- 
erwise unknown attacks that would be too bizarre for a mere mortal 
to easily imagine without machinic help. There were of course com- 
putational limits in practice: These automated proof-systems were 
typically sound but not complete, and also often required interac- 
tion with a human to guide the heuristics used by the model-checker. 
Generally a human had to describe the model of the protocol and its 
security properties using whatever formal language was supported 
by the theorem prover before putting its automation to work. The 
early results were promising: A new attack was found on the vener- 
able Needham–Schroeder key exchange protocol 17 years after the 
protocol was published [ 61 ], and this attack could be formalized us- 
ing an automated theorem-prover based on the well-known Com- 
municating Sequential Processes formalism [ 54 ]. The hope was that 
while Needham–Schroeder was a tiny protocol composed of only 
three lines of code, automated methods could be used to prove the se- 
curity of very complex protocols like Transport Layer Security (TLS) 
that were based on thousands of lines of code. With the use of the 
higher-order logic-based proof-prover Isabelle [ 62 ], the very complex 
protocol TLS was also proven to be secure [ 63 ]. As a result, the tri- 
umph of formalism in cryptography seemed complete, and a bright 
future of cryptography—and its wider application in complex secu- 
rity protocols—seemed to be inexorable. 
The question of what it means for a cryptosystem to be secure 
against an adversary is a philosophical question embodied as a tech- 
nical question. First, if the larger philosophical notion of secure com- 
munication could be reduced to the sending of an encrypted message 
between secret keepers using a cryptosystem such that a known-class 
of adversaries could not decrypt the message, then in principle secu- 
rity could be reduced to a matter of mathematical proof. So while 
Shannon proved no adversary can break an information-theoretic 
and perfectly secure cryptosystem, where the key is never reused, 
in practice, these new results proved breaking a cryptosystem with 
key reusage is practically impossible if the adversary has a bounded 
amount of time and computational power. The initial application of 
computational complexity theory and logic-based formal verification 
made huge strides in solving some of these difficult questions for both 
cryptosystems and their usage in the authentication logics for access 
control systems. However, the philosophical question of whether or 
not there were fundamental limits to formal abstraction would show 
that questions of metaphysics and ontology had consequences for 
preserving secrets against adversaries. 
The limits of formalization in cryptography 
All was not well for formalism; systems had to be implemented by 
working code to be usable by secret keepers, but the capabilities of 
adversaries were not limited by any formal model. First, a number 
of protocols that were proven to be cryptographically secure in the- 
ory were shown to be insecure when implemented in practice [ 64 ]. 
The famous provably secure R S A padding proof was shown to have 
an error in the proof [ 65 ]. Worse, there was a brutal attack on R S A, 
which took advantage of TLS servers telling clients about improper 
padding, which could then be used to decrypt their encrypted traf- 
fic [ 36 ], despite the core of TLS being shown to be formally secure 
[ 63 ]. There was further worry that if protocols were proven secure 
under the Random Oracle assumption, they were not actually secure 
in practice as a hash function could be a poor approximation of a 
random oracle. After all, a random oracle is not even physically re- 
alizable by a finite computer, as it would require a description of in- 
finite length; its underlying idea is that given infinite possible inputs, 
each input produces a random output independent from every other 
possible random output [ 66 ]. 
Despite the considerable success of formalization, the issue of 
concrete parameters also was still more guess-work than a proper 
science. For elements of cryptography like keys, bigger was always 
better: Yet, exactly what key size was reasonable to resist an adver- 
sary like the NSA with supercomputing resources? The move away 
from R S A to more efficient elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) exem- 
plified concerns as it was not even clear which curve to use. Despite 
formal proofs, in practice security was based more on an oral tradi- 
tion than a science as “the decision about what kind of curve to use 
in ECC is a subjective one based on the user’s best guess about future 
vulnerabilities” [ 67 ]. The debate over the actual properties delivered 
by provable security came to a head when the inventor of elliptic 
curve cryptography [ 68 ], Neal Koblitz published an infamous paper 
with Alfred Menezes called Another Look at Provable Security that 
claimed “One has to wonder how many ‘proofs’ of security are ever 
read carefully with a critical eye” [ 69 ]. As they note, Mihir Bellare 
and Phil Rogaway’s proof of optimal padding for R S A “went essen- 
tially unexamined for 7 years,” so “one cannot help asking whether 
the lengthy and often poorly written “proofs” of less famous secu- 
rity claims are ever read carefully by anyone” [ 69 ]. Although one 
would hope a computer could verify these proofs, automated the- 
orem provers effectively reduced the security of a protocol to the 
underlying—and perhaps flawed—model-checking software, which 
could be sound, but nonetheless was not complete and could not 
handle the complex assumptions of most proofs. The epistemologi- 
cal question of whether these computer-generated proofs could even 
be checked and trusted could not be banished [ 70 ]. Automated the- 
orem provers produced proofs that were typically so complex and 
long that it would be impractical for any human to bother to check 
their correctness [ 71 ]. If the cryptographic research community did 
not regularly check important and concise proofs produced by its 
own members, what were the chances of their checking the tedious 
proofs generated by machines? 
Despite building a large-case against the nature of reductionist 
security proofs in cryptography being error-prone and even circular 
[ 72 ], most academic cryptographers believed that abandoning secu- 
rity definitions and cryptographic proofs would be a step backwards. 
Otherwise, cryptography would be based on subjective intuition, and 
so prevented from being based on rigorous and scientific grounds 
[ 73 ]. This debate over the status of proofs within cryptography had 
a philosophical parallel in an earlier debate between the formalist 
and intuitionist approaches to the status of proofs in the foundations 
of mathematics. This philosophical debate arose out of the failure of 
Russell and Whitehead to construct the foundations of mathematics 
on logic [ 74 ]. For the formalists, what was important was that a set 
of axioms created a system of objects, and for each new object there 
can be a reduction in a consistent manner to the underlying model

approach to security is an unacceptable path for most members of 
the cryptographic research community, and certainly the general

The question of the status of proofs can thus be restated in terms 
of the reality of mathematical objects. Are mathematical proofs real 
objects in terms of metaphysics? It would seem that mathematical 
objects could be as ontologically real as the rest of our world, or 
at least real structural regularities in our world [ 79 ]. While it ap- 
pears feasible that one could claim that a perfect circle is some sort 
of Platonic object about which a mathematician could have a pri- 
ori knowledge, it seems absurd to claim that Needham–Schroeder 
key agreement or R S A padding schemes have some sort of neces- 
sary ontological existence that precedes their invention. As noted by 
Rogaway [ 80 ], cryptographic programs are literally constructed by 
human engineers and cryptographers. Therefore, some variant of so- 
cial constructivism over the nature of mathematical objects used to 
study cryptography would naturally fit the work of the cryptographic 
research community, which could then vary in opinion over the meta- 
physical status of these objects and their proofs [ 81 ]. 
However, there are dangers with replacing the objective reality 
with social consensus: “The cost is that “objectivity” is reinterpreted 
as “social,”and although it still refers to something external to the in- 
dividual it is no longer external to humankind” [ 81 ]. As per Lakatos, 
proofs are then constructed by all-too-human scientific communities 
who, while adversarial over the details, are united by their common 
desire for knowledge about their subject matter [ 82 ]. In cryptogra- 
phy, there is no common humankind, or even a unified research com- 
munity; the common understanding of the social as put forward in 
the philosophy of science is a cooperative scientific community, but in 
cryptography humans are divided into secret keepers and adversaries. 
Thus, for a system to be secure against an adversary, it requires more 
than social consensus: The system must hold up its properties against 
full-blooded adversaries actively trying to undermine and find flaws 
in the work of the community of cryptographic researchers. More 
suitable is the approach of Daniel Dennett, who argues that math- 
ematics, science, and even engineering can discover “real patterns”
that are causally efficacious in the world, and so can be helpful in ac- 
complishing evolutionary goals while avoiding Platonism and more 
stringent versions of scientific realism [ 83 ]. A cryptosystem may be 
socially constructed, but it may nonetheless discover and exploit a 
real pattern in the world; it must work in detecting and creating 
ontological regularities in the world that are independent of agents, 
whether or not they are secret keepers or adversaries. Whether or not 
something is secure then is a matter of its survival in the real world 
against adversaries, rather than against attacks made by the scientific 
community within an austere and pristine formal framework. Rather 
than being an arbitrator of the status of security, the cryptographic 
research community can be thought of as a testing ground—or, better 
yet, a collective laboratory—for new primitives and protocols. 
Proofs thus provide an important role as “intuition-pumps”
of the research community in finding weaknesses in security be- 
fore a new cryptographic construction is created and in repair- 
ing already-existing cryptography in the wild [ 84 ]. The proofs pro- 
duced by mathematicians—and accepted by the larger mathematical 
community—are often quite informal if not rigorous [ 85 ], and would 
require further transformations to count as a proof in a formalized 
logical system [ 86 ]. While many proofs are indeed informal and intu- 
itive in cryptography, the historical tendency has been to move in the 
other direction by constructing increasingly rigorous logical proofs, 
including the aid of automatic theorem provers to detect unknown 
adversarial behavior. The very reliance on computing machinery to 
make proofs further undermines the claim that these proofs are a sort 
of Platonic a priori knowledge, rather than being quasi-empirical dis- 
coveries [ 70 ]. This positions cryptography as a discipline more akin 
to engineering or science than a relative of pure mathematics. 
The received view is that engineering emanates historically from 
the application of science [ 87 ]. With cryptography and computer sci- 
ence as a whole, it is more often than not the other way around: 
Concrete engineering practices precede the slow work of scientific 
systematization in cryptography. This does not mean that the objects 
created by engineering are not real, but precisely the opposite: cryp- 
tography is a kind of philosophical engineering that is creating new 
ontologies with novel properties [ 88 ]. The new ontologies embod- 
ied by cryptographic primitives and protocols, whose security and 
weaknesses outstrip the intended design of their inventors, demand 
that mathematical and empirical work be done to determine the exact 
properties of these engineered artifacts in the wild. This is precisely 
because these embodied programs function in the real world while 
also being designed in the abstract with only a partial model of the 
world in the minds of their creators [ 89 ]. So cryptography can be 
considered as both designing new objects via engineering based on 
idealized models and making scientific, empirical discoveries about 
the operations—and security—of these objects in the real world. 
The model is only useful in clarifying aspects of the entire sys- 
tem and so abstracts away from the rich details of their underlying 
subject matter. This is not only an issue for cryptography, as all for- 
mal models abstract from the real world. Even Hilbert and Bernays, 
the founders of the formalism, recognized that the work of mathe- 
maticians operates “predominately with theories which do not repro- 
duce the actual state of affairs completely, but represent a simplifying 
idealization of the state of affairs and have their meaning therein”
[ 75 ]. The problem with relying on purely formal models for security 
properties is that, as Cantwell Smith puts is: “Mathematics is unique, 
with respect to models, because (at least to a first level of approxima- 
tion) its subject matter is the world of models and abstract structures, 
and therefore the model–world relationship is relatively unproblem- 
atic” [ 5 ]. 
The subject matter of formal models in cryptography, therefore, 
is not mathematics—as would be the case in meta-mathematics—but 
rather an engineered system facing an adversary. The limits of for- 
malization in cryptography are due to the model of a system being 
mistaken for the system itself as embedded in the world, which in- 
cludes the social behavior of secret keepers. Likewise, a model of an 
adversary cannot be mistaken for the actual adversary, which could 
possess attacks unconstrained by any model. The root of the prob- 
lem was that formal definitions that undergirded the entire field of 
provable security: the definitions were idealizations of reality, which 
failed to take into account important aspects of the implementa-

The philosophical question of the limits of formalism is of prime 
importance to cryptography. Debates within cryptography echo de- 
bates earlier in the philosophy of mathematics, ranging from debates 
over whether mathematics provides privileged access to the nature to 
reality of whether or not the metaphysical complexity of the world 
outstrips any formal model of the world [ 5 ]. If there are indeed funda- 
mental limits to correctness in computers, then even automated the-

erties would hold in the real world [ 89 ]. As Ross Anderson notes in 
his seminal paper Why Crypto-systems Fail , the engineers who con-

to prove their security properties “have suffered from a lack of infor- 
mation about how their products fail in practice, as opposed to how 
they might fail in theory,” as an adversary would typically not reveal 
that they had broken a cryptosystem [ 91 ]. The reason for cryptog- 
raphy’s lack of the virtuous feedback cycle of other scientific disci- 
plines and engineering research fields was simple: secret keepers that 
reveal their secrets due to broken cryptosystems may also be loathe 
to reveal their failure or not even know that their secret has been 
revealed. 
The adversary revealed 
The limits of formalization are revealed when the capabilities of an 
adversary are not captured by a formal model. While the notion of 
an adversary had been formalized with increasingly sophisticated ab- 
stractions, the details of how actual nation-state level adversaries 
worked remained speculation. With the leaks by Edward Snowden, 
the unthinkable happened: the raw secret techniques of the most 
powerful adversary yet imagined were revealed to the general pub- 
lic. This guiding example of the limits of formalization was an event 
of historic proportions to the field of cryptography as, precisely be- 
cause the NSA was the most powerful adversary imaginable in terms 
of cryptography, academics had long-used the NSA as a sort of mythi- 
cal adversary in their research papers. If a technique could defeat the 
power of the NSA, it was surmized to be able to withstand the at- 
tacks of any lesser adversary. Before Snowden’s leaks, although the 
academic cryptographic research community knew that the NSA had 
long suppressed the release of public key cryptography, their precise 
capabilities as an adversary could only be guessed [ 23 ]. For exam- 
ple, the discovery of a large secret server farm under construction in 
Utah was surmized to be a cryptanalytic facility aimed at the “brute- 
forcing” of private key material, at least judging by its massive size 
and its energy consumption [ 92 ]. The Snowden leaks finally offered 
a glimpse into what a large-scale adversary with thousands of em- 
ployed secret keepers and more than a billion dollars in budget was 
capable of. Although the number of secrets leaked by Snowden are 
clearly far from exhaustive and rapidly dating, we will touch upon a 
number of findings that revealed blindspots in the prior philosophical 
framing of cryptography [ 93 ]. 
The first surprise was that defeating cryptography via breaking 
a cryptosystem was not the primary methodology employed by a 
real-world adversary to reveal secrets. Although the NSA was active 
in cryptanalysis, there was no evidence of their having hidden revo- 
lutionary cryptographic techniques to break existing cryptosystems: 
The polynomial adversary did seem to be an accurate assessment of 
the NSA’s cryptanalytic capabilities, and even the NSA could not de- 
feat modern cryptography. Years earlier it had been shown that the 
NSA had discovered and deployed differential cryptanalysis, a pow- 
erful mathematical technique capable of breaking early cryptosys- 
tems, before the technique had been discovered by the cryptographic 
academics [ 94 ]. Instead of cryptanalysis, the NSA was obsessed with 
obtaining secret key material via attacking parts of the system left 
out of standard information-theoretic model of cryptographic com- 
munication from Shannon onwards. Why would an adversary attack 
the encrypted messages when it could more easily attack the secret 
keepers themselves? Often key material was stored insecurely by the 
sender or receiver of a secret and could be extracted by attacking 
the secret keeper’s device via malware (as shown by “Foxacid”) [ 95 ]. 
Rather than attack the message, the communication channel itself 
could be subverted; such malware could even be deployed against 
the communication channel itself in order to access sensitive data, as 
shown by an attack the British Government Communications Head- 
quarters (GCHQ) did against a Belgian telecommunications provider 
in order to obtain secret information from their nominal allies [ 95 ]. 
Implementation details left out of abstract models of protocols were 
ripe for attack: The NSA had a particular focus on subverting the 
TLS protocol used to secure access to websites. One technique, called 
“Quantum Insertion,” was simply injecting fake packets into an oth- 
erwise seemingly secure TLS connection in order to redirect an agent 
toward a malicious website of the attacker’s choosing [ 95 ]. Lastly, the 
point-to-point model of communication used since Shannon did not 
take into account massive trusted third-party platforms that medi- 
ated most communication channels. Via the PRISM programme, the 
NSA was revealed to have access to Microsoft, Google, Facebook, 
Apple, and other large centralized platforms, including access to the 
plaintext of emails and web data. 
The second finding was that the simplest primitives used in the 
reductions for security proofs could be attacked far more easily than 
the protocols itself. Via Project Bullrun, the NSA had a budget of 
250 million dollars to subvert cryptographic standards [ 96 ]. This was 
no surprise, as standards like DSA for digital signatures, created by 
NIST but designed by the NSA, were clearly crippled to the extent 
that DSA was both inefficient and could only be used for authenti- 
cation, not confidentiality [ 23 ]. On the one hand, major standards 
by NIST long-rumored to be weakened by the NSA—such as the 
symmetric key encryption of AES—were shown not to be broken 
by the NSA in the Snowden leaks. On the other hand, Snowden con- 
firmed some long-standing suspicions about the NSA subversion of 
the NIST Dual_EC_DRBG (Dual Elliptic Curve Deterministic Ran- 
dom Bit Generator) standard [ 96 ], which was also internationalized 
by the International Standards Organization [ 97 ]. Cryptosystems as- 
sume that the keys are generated via random number generators, but 
a subtle “backdoor” was put in the random number generation so 
that even though its output appeared random, the possessor of the 
backdoor information could predict the generated keys. This inse- 
cure random number generation meant that TLS connections using 
it could be compromised. The NSA even worked with vendors—
such as the R S A company, although without the knowledge of Ron 
Rivest and his coinventors—to ensure placement of the insecure 
random number generator in high-value corporate infrastructures 
[ 98 ]. This shocked the cryptographic research community, and Rivest 
himself was surprised at the poor judgment of his own company. Fur- 
thermore, this strategy of having deliberately weakened standards 
or products with backdoors could easily be applied to other crypto-

The last finding of the Snowden leaks was that the patterns of 
messages given by secret keepers was just as important, if not more 
so, to the adversary than the encrypted message itself. While the most 
fundamental problem in cryptography is the protection of secret in- 
formation being sent from a sender to a receiver, another even more 
difficult problem is how to send a message without revealing the iden- 
tity of the sender and receiver to an adversary. In other words, how 
can one hide not just the data of the encrypted message, but also the 
metadata of who is communicating with whom? Techniques to do 
this would unlink the sender and the receiver of a message so that they 
can communicate anonymously [ 100 ]. In short, the pattern of com- 
munication revealed the information organization of the secret keep- 
ers to the adversary, and keeping the organization’s structure secret 
was itself more important in many cases than the secrets themselves. 
Take for example the organizers of a protest in a country under au- 
thoritarian rule, who must keep the information about the time and 
location of the protest hidden, but also their own identities to escape 
arrest. Even if the message was encrypted, often the encrypted mes- 
sage has metadata that is itself plaintext on the level of the protocol 
implementation, such as the name of the website being visited using 
TLS. The monitoring of messages, including encrypted messages, for 
patterns in communication by an adversary is called traffic analysis , 
and as Diffie and Landau put it: “Traffic analysis, not cryptanalysis, 
is the backbone of communications intelligence” [ 15 ]. The Snowden 
leaks showed that the NSA was a master of traffic analysis: the slo- 
gan of the NSA was “Collect it all, know it all, exploit it all” [ 93 ]. 
With millions upon millions of messages collected everyday across 
almost every country, the NSA could do large-scale data analysis and 
visualization of social networks via programs like “Boundless Infor- 
mant” and personalized monitoring in real-time via “Keyscore-X”
[ 101 ]. VPNs and anonymous communication networks such as Tor 
deliver messages from sender to receiver in a first-in, first-out man- 
ner through a series of decentralized relays [ 102 ]. Existing real-world 
techniques were insufficient: VPNs and even Tor were vulnerable to 
traffic analysis by a powerful adversary such as the NSA that could 
capture and record every message, although deanonymizing Tor users 
took considerable manual work by the NSA due to its decentralized 
nature [ 103 ]. 
What sort of research problems were provoked by the NSA 
that went beyond the prior formal frameworks? The Snowden leaks 
shocked not only the general public, but many members of the cryp- 
tographic research community to their core. One of the founders of 
provable security, Phil Rogaway, wrote an eloquent call to arms, en- 
titled The Mor al Char acter of Cryptographic Work , where he said: 
“As computer scientists and cryptographers, we are twice culpable 
when it comes to mass surveillance: computer science created the 
technologies that underlie our communications infrastructure, and 
that are now turning it into an apparatus for surveillance and con- 
trol; while cryptography contains within it the underused potential 
to help redirect this tragic turn”[ 104 ]. Having the adversary revealed 
caused a transformation of the paradigm of the field of cryptography, 
at least for researchers like Rogaway [ 105 ]. Although the majority of 
the cryptographic research community continued with little thought 
to real-world implementation or concerns, a portion of the crypto- 
graphic research community did work to resolve the most egregious 
attacks brought to light by Snowden. New protocols like Signal were 
created for secure messaging that featured new properties like for- 
ward secrecy , where the compromise of a key did not allow the adver- 
sary to decrypt all past and even future messages [ 106 ]. The ancient 
TLS protocol was finally properly analyzed, and new techniques in 
formal verification allowed the model-checking software to go be- 
yond the mere analysis of a model to actually generate code that was 
verified to embody the model [ 107 ]. Focus on key storage in hard- 
ware that was difficult to extract via secure enclaves also became a 
major focus of work [ 108 ], as did the logging of key material to pre- 
vent forgeries (including for TLS) [ 109 ] and the decentralization of 
information away from large platforms, leading eventually to interest 
in blockchain-based systems to preserve the integrity of information 
against adversarial and centralized systems [ 110 ]. 
Due to the Snowden leaks, the field of provable security had to 
revisit its formal assumptions: the NSA subversion of cryptographic 
primitives and basic protocols was a thorny problem to address. 
Suspicions naturally fell on the NIST-selected elliptic curves, whose 
precise choice of parameters were difficult to publicly verify, and 
which seemed less than ideal in terms of efficiency. Daniel Bern- 
stein and Tanja Lange created an alternative with the peer-reviewed 
Curve25519 curve that was even more efficient than the standard- 
ized NIST curves, and it soon found its way into major standards 
like TLS [ 111 ]. Another research paradigm, called malicious secu- 
rity , had been explored earlier in terms of viruses and malware by 
a small group of cryptographers to determine if a protocol could be 
made secure even if the fundamental primitives to which proofs re- 
duced security were subverted [ 112 ]. In the wake of the possibility of 
standardized primitives being subverted, this line of research could 
then be applied to the assumptions of modern cryptographic proto- 
cols [ 113 ]. 
Traditional cryptographic research did not focus on enabling 
anonymous communication, and what little research existed on 
anonymous communication was woefully underdeveloped [ 114 ]. Al- 
though the field of cryptography had been well-developed mathe- 
matically, the study of anonymity still seemed stuck at the presci- 
entific stage of determining a basic terminology and methodology 
[ 100 ]. While work in epistemic logic can formalize anonymity, it has 
not been united with other models [ 115 ]. In contrast to traditional 
work in cryptography, work in anonymity consists of hiding meta- 
data details, like timing and volume information of encrypted mes- 
sages. However, widely used anonymous communication networks 
such as Tor did not protect against a global passive adversary that 
could watch and copy every single message on a network [ 102 ]. Prior 
to the Snowden leaks, this kind of adversary was considered unreal- 
istic, but post-Snowden it became clear this kind of mass data col- 
lection was already possible. This led to renewed interest in mix net- 
working, a technique invented by David Chaum in which encrypted 
messages were shuffled like a deck of cards in a series of decentralized 
servers, and so delivered to receivers in a different order than sent, 
effectively anonymizing the message [ 114 ]. After laying dormant for 
almost 30 years, mixnets like Nym were developed to prevent the 
traffic analysis of users, exemplifying the next step in protocol devel- 
opment beyond TLS [ 116 ].

powers is still unknown as the Snowden leaks gave only a glimpse 
of their cryptanalytic power at a particular point in time. A further 
paradigm shift awaits, as the invention of a real-world quantum com- 
puter, by implementing Shor’s algorithm to exponentially parallelize 
factoring, might break the fundamental trap door assumptions that

The Snowden leaks pulled the mask off the NSA, showing that 
powerful global passive adversaries and active attacks on all aspects 
of communication are a realistic threat model. From a philosophi- 
cal perspective, the Snowden leaks provide an example of how both 
our initial philosophical framework can analyze an adversary like 
the NSA, while demonstrating the limits of various abstract models, 
for example, in capturing the NSA’s actual capabilities. Of course, 
being an adversary is a relative notion: For the NSA, Snowden him- 
self was the adversary that leaked their secret techniques. Snowden’s 
leaks also brought to popular attention a political and philosophi- 
cal shift: cryptography was historically a weapon of defense used by 
large-scale information organizations of secret keepers such as gov- 
ernments, yet now tools such as big data analysis and the exploita- 
tion of security flaws were focused on individuals in the general pop- 
ulation, even indiscriminately in the case of mass surveillance. The 
adversaries were also changing. On the one hand, individuals were 
increasingly empowered to guard their own secrets with cryptogra- 
phy due to the invention of public key cryptography. On the other 
hand, the attacks that were once considered only the province of the 
NSA, such as brute-force attacks and malware, increasingly became 
usable by not only other intelligence agencies, but also a host of non- 
state actors ranging from criminal networks to private organizations. 
The successful formalization of the adversary had effectively caused 
cryptography to ignore important aspects that fell outside this model: 
attacks on the communication channel and secret keepers themselves, 
the subversion of cryptographic primitives, and the statistical anal- 
ysis of the social network of secret keepers. The kernel of a future 
research agenda had been revealed. 
Conclusion: a research agenda 
Philosophy has long been the handmaiden of science, yet in the case 
of cryptography the question of the adversary seems to reveal a la- 
cuna at the heart of philosophy as well. One should not forget that 
the translation of “Satan” in Hebrew is adversary, and “program- 
ming Satan’ s computer”—where the very channel to carry communi- 
cations can be malicious—is one of the most difficult tasks imagin- 
able [ 119 ]. Politics has long been the realm of adversarial behavior, 
but now—as shown by the Snowden leaks and the increased ubiq- 
uity of attacks on digital communication of all sorts—the problem of 
communicating in the presence of adversaries is also a technical ques- 
tion. This technical question impacts not only governments and other 
information organizations, but nearly all of humanity as we increas- 
ingly become dependent on the Internet. And, as has been shown by 
our analysis of cryptography, any technology is built on a robust, if 
implicit, philosophical foundation that is worthy of study in its own 
right. 
While cryptography has come quite a long way in defending com- 
munications, the purpose of a philosophy of cryptography should be 
2-fold: first, to make philosophical insights legible to cryptographers; 
and second, insofar as information theory has served as the funda- 
mental building block of the philosophy of cognitive science, artificial 
intelligence, and even the newly founded philosophy of information, 
there is no reason why philosophers should not apply their philo- 
sophical machinery to cryptography. Rather than view cryptography 
as a pristine, if not bizarre, branch of mathematics where philoso- 
phers have to be called in to tidy up the foundations, it would be 
better to use the insights provided by philosophy to point towards 
ways of expanding cryptography into a more general science of se- 
curity. 
In order to qualify as a science, abstraction must be tempered 
by empirical research in order to allow secure communication in re- 
ality, not just on pen and paper. So far the progress of cryptogra- 
phy has critically depended since Shannon on the advancement of 
abstraction, yet future progress may require—in order to not mis- 
take the map for the territory per Korzybski—the rev er se of ab- 
straction: reconciliation with the underlying subject matter. Cantwell 
Smith defines reconciliation as “filling in the details” of any theory 
[ 5 ]. This could allow us to revisit our abstractions and choices to 
produce more accurate models that do justice to their underlying 
subject matter, whether in terms of predictions or even just explana- 
tions. We suggest the following four questions for a research agenda 
to reconcile the drive towards abstraction in cryptography with its 
embedding in the world, where each question is to be explored in 
more detail. 
(1) Can we study dynamic social networks of secret communica- 
tion modeled on the empirical behavior of secret keepers? 
(2) By incorporating statistics, can new models of the adversary 
take into account more accurate data about their capabilities? 
(3) Can cryptography incorporate interdisciplinary methods and 
insights that can capture the complexity of secure communica- 
tion? 
(4) Will philosophy let us make explicit and then reformulate the 
hidden assumptions of cryptographic communication, and vice 
v er sa ? 
Fir st, can w e stud y d ynamic social netw or ks of secret communica- 
tion modeled on the social behavior of secret k eeper s? As initially ex- 
plored by access control and authentication logics, the question of the 
secret is a question of information organization. Adversaries are not 
limited to technical means, they may actively be attempting to reveal 
secrets of their targets by intercepting the message, but also monitor- 
ing or interfering with—or even subverting—the behavior of secret 
keepers. Today, information organization is thought of as a social 
network rather than a simplified centralized organization. It is at this 
point, we should note the limits of Shannon’s simplification of secure 
communication is insufficient: as demonstrated, albeit negatively, in 
spades by Snowden’s insider leak of one of the world’s most powerful 
organizations of secret keepers, information organization is a norm- 
governed social relationship between secret keepers and their secret 
that prevents this secret from escaping their agreed-upon bounds and 
so falling into the hands of the adversary. In the traditional model of 
Shannon, using conventions developed in the original treatment of 
R S A [ 29 ], there is only one channel of communication between two 
agents Alice and Bob, with a single malicious adversary—known as 
Eve or Mallory—who is attempting to maliciously disrupt or eaves- 
drop upon the communication, with some perhaps backup cast of 
trusted third parties or other infrastructure so that this model would 
hold by induction to any number of channels.

cryptography to go beyond simple maxims from philosophy and so-

are loathe to share potentially embarrassing data [ 121 ]. The collec-

common goal for all parties to increase their own security by learning 
from the lessons of others. 
Second, by incorporating real-world data, can new models of the 
adv er sary tak e into account more accurate data about their capabili- 
ties? One of the most pernicious problems facing cryptography is an 
accurate description of the adversary. While the abstractions such as 
the polynomial–time adversary and the Dolev–Yao adversary have 
been historically useful, they are far too abstract for constructing re- 
alistic adversarial models [ 34 ,52 ]. Adversaries are also not a simple 
universal enemy, but may be constituted by real-world networks be- 
tween humans and machines, and they may not be operating alone 
but rather in coalition with other adversaries. Understanding these 
adversaries in excruciating detail will require both new kinds of logic 
and models that are informed by empirical data, which in turn will 
require an incorporation of statistical methods. For an example in the 
context of mix networking, statistical techniques like Monte–Carlo 
simulation allow us to define the probability of an local adversary 
linking a particular message to a sender or receiver even when the 
adversary has only a partial view of an entire network [ 122 ]. In terms 
of logic, more detailed logical models from distributed systems and 
the semantics of information theory, which can potentially line up 
with the behavior of these models as complex systems, have been un- 
derexploited in cryptography [ 123 ,124 ]. What will be needed is not 
less abstraction, but a greater variety of abstractions and mathemat- 
ical tools to effectively deal with the real-world data of adversarial 
communication. 
The political economy of the surveillance powers of an adversary 
should also be taken into account. For example, in the “Five Eyes”
alliance as leaked by Snowden [ 125 ], the USA operates with alliance 
with Canada, the UK, and other Anglophone countries by sharing 
intelligence capabilities. Some parts of this coalition may have spe- 
cialized capabilities, such as the tremendous computing facilities of 
the USA, while others like the UK’s GCHQ may be capable of trap- 
ping undersea cables in Europe, a task that was outside of the rules 
of the USA at the time. Not all members of an adversarial coalition 
are equal; coalitions may also have different levels of access to ca- 
pabilities, such as the ability to ask others for surveillance capabili- 
ties but not being able to autonomously deploy that capability: for 
example, Saudi Arabia will be forced to choose between using Chi- 
nese and American capabilities for surveillance, leading Saudi Ara- 
bia’s infrastructure to be a large extent subject to a form of cyber- 
colonialism [ 126 ]. However, other countries like Iran may chose to 
obtain their surveillance capabilities from the Russia–China nexus. 
These adversarial coalitions are not necessarily limited to nation- 
states, but may be adversarial coalitions within nation-states: for ex- 
ample, surveillance capabilities are typically not shared directly be- 
tween the NSA and FBI in the USA, although the NSA needs to alert 
the FBI if they encounter information relevant to a domestic inves- 
tigation. More commonly, coalitions may be between superempow- 
ered individuals and loose social networks with ever blurring bor- 
ders, such as the hacking campaigns of Anonymous [ 127 ]. Adver- 
sarial coalitions between nation-states and individuals allow them to 
combine forces (as often is seen in Russian hacking) and state-level 
capabilities may target individuals and other nonstate actors, as ex- 
emplified by North Korean malicious malware to obtain financial re- 
sources for their nuclear program [ 128 ]. These coalitions may have 
vastly different access to inside information, cryptanalytic abilities, 
computational power, and more. Therefore, in order to turn cyber- 
security from an art to an empirically driven science, the first com- 
mandment should be to know the adversary: to create as an effective 
and complete model as possible of the real-world capabilities of the 
adversary. 
Third, can cryptography incorporate interdisciplinary methods 
and insights that can capture the complexity of secure communica- 
tion? Information flows between dynamic networks lend themselves 
to the theory of complex systems [ 129 ]. With suitable fine-grained 
descriptions of the communication channels between the secret keep- 
ers and the adversaries, invariants may be observed and predictions 
made while retaining fidelity to the underlying the subject matter. 
For any given description of a dynamic communication network, 
the behavior and evolution of this network can be described over 
time at various scales, with possible trajectories outlined due to ad- 
versarial attacks [ 120 ]. As is characteristic of complex systems, ex- 
treme attacks by the adversary may result in entire phase shifts in 
the structure of the communication network. Within this matrix of 
interdisciplinary work around complex adaptive systems, the social 
embedding of cryptography can be fruitfully studied [ 130 ]. In the 
emerging fields of network dynamics and nonparametric statistics—
as most of the statistics will be done over social structures that 
infamously exhibit “power law” distributions—progress in model- 
ing may be made, and research could profit from, similar work in 
studying the dynamics of information flow in related fields like epi- 
demics [ 121 ]. With enough data and rich-enough models, this work 
could allow data-driven simulations to reveal possible invariants in 
terms of the preservation of secrecy. Yet, simulation-driven work has 
its own philosophical quandaries, particularly if the models reach 
a level of such complexity that their results are epistemologically 
opaque: a scientific invariant may be evident and have predictive 
power, but the reasons for it may not be known [ 131 ]. Complex mod- 
els should always then be simplified and pruned, and then refined 
to not become too heavyweight. The theoretical apparatus of cryp- 
tography will naturally need to expand by incorporating rigorous 
approaches from economics, psychology, and philosophy that em- 
brace complexity. This would transform cryptography into a general- 
purpose science of adversarial behavior, which could be called a 
science of information security, otherwise known as cybersecurity 
[ 132 ].

vironment find themselves increasingly difficult to hold. This should 
lead to the questioning of Shannon’s foundational model of secrets 
being sent over channels between discrete agents, as explored in our 
own preliminary philosophical investigation into cryptography. As 
shown by the Extended Mind Hypothesis, intelligence is distributed 
between the cognizing agent and the environment, and so agents may 
have fluid boundaries that dynamically integrate parts of their envi- 
ronment, including their digital environment [ 133 ]. Still, rarely have 
attacks on our increasingly vital digital infrastructure been taken as 
seriously as physical attacks [ 134 ], despite our cognitive infrastruc- 
ture and our social institutions being increasingly digital. There is 
also much to be learned by philosophy from cryptography. Indeed, 
all sorts of questions from ontology to epistemology can be recast in 
explicit adversarial modes. It was such adversarial debate that gave 
birth to philosophy via Socrates, and philosophy should revisit the 
adversary as its foundational principle. 
The philosophy of cryptography is the philosophical analysis 
of increasingly rigorous abstract models of adversaries, where this 
abstraction came at the cost of blinding cryptography to the very 
real capabilities of the adversary that were outside these models. The 
Snowden leaks brought some of the adversarial capabilities of the 
NSA to popular attention, and today there are many adversaries with 
diverse, if not even more powerful, adversarial powers due to the 
progress of technology. For example, the utilization of the mass col- 
lection of surveillance-based data by artificial intelligence is already 
underway, which will lead to increasingly powerful adversaries that 
can try to predict, control, and manipulate humans. A philosophical 
analysis of cryptography can point to a research agenda to reconcile 
these abstractions with the scientific, epistemological, and even 
ontological concerns brought to the forefront by the metaphysical 
richness of the world. It was a historic accident that the internet 
was designed in a trusted academic environment, and yet today we 
are not only universally connected, but universally vulnerable to 
adversaries at a global scale. Historically, the presence of adversaries 
has been a driver of biological evolution and social history, and 
so it is through secure communication and the organization of 
information that we as a species are likely to steer through global 
crisis. A world without adversaries would be utopian, but also static. 
A world inhabited by adversaries is not only a dangerous world, but 
one of dynamism and growth. In this regard, philosophy should be 
grateful for the adversary.
